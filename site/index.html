Worker reallocation · Developing economies · 2015 to 2019

The talent developing economies lose fastest is AI talent

Job postings and employment show where demand is going. Migration shows where the workers go. Using the World Bank and LinkedIn public talent migration data, here is the reallocation layer underneath the employment trends.

Benjamin Reyes · Data science and AI engineering · Data: World Bank Group & LinkedIn

01 · Which skills leave

AI skills drain fastest, and they flow straight to high-income economies

Net migration rates are measured within each skill: arrivals minus departures, per 10,000 LinkedIn members who hold that skill. In developing economies, every software, data and AI skill group below leaves faster than the typical non-tech skill. AI leaves fastest, at roughly three times the non-tech rate, while the same skills accumulate in high-income economies.

02 · A sorting story

Tech talent amplifies whatever reallocation is already underway

Each dot is a country. Horizontally, how fast its non-tech talent moves; vertically, how fast its tech talent moves. The cloud runs steeper than the diagonal: countries that lose workers lose their tech workers faster, and countries that gain workers gain tech workers faster. Tech skills leave faster than non-tech skills in developing economies.

This is less a story of brain drain than of high-mobility workers sorting toward the locations where their skills earn the most.

03 · The trend

In four years, most developing economies flipped to net losers

Summing each country's bilateral flows gives its total net migration per 10,000 members. In 2015 the median economy in every income group was a small net gainer. By 2019 most developing economies were losing talent on net, while high-income economies kept gaining.

04 · Which industries bleed

The losses concentrate in health, education and knowledge services

The industries losing talent fastest in developing economies are the ones that build human capital: health, education, professional services and ICT. Real estate, administrative services, retail and finance lose the least. Every one of these sectors gains talent in the median high-income economy.

05 · Where they go

North America and Western Europe absorb most of it

For each developing economy, I took the partners it loses talent to on net and computed each one's share of that loss, then averaged across countries. Language and geography shape the routes: Latin America to the United States and Spain, francophone Africa and the Maghreb to France and Canada, South Asia to the United States, Australia and the Gulf.

Not every route runs north. Venezuelan talent moves to Ecuador, Colombia and Peru, and Indian talent to Nepal, Rwanda and Sudan.

Notes

Data and caveats

Source and definitions

World Bank Group and LinkedIn, Digital Data for Development: Talent Migration, public use file (dataset 0038044). Migration is recorded when a member's job location changes country. Net means arrivals minus departures, normalised by LinkedIn members in the country, industry or skill and multiplied by 10,000. Developing economies are those the file classifies as low, lower middle or upper middle income: .

What this data can and cannot say
  • The latest public refresh ends in 2019, and each year is a trailing three-year average. Pandemic-era remote work and the generative AI wave are not captured.
  • LinkedIn over-represents urban, white-collar and knowledge-sector workers. These are flows of LinkedIn-visible talent, not total migration.
  • Countries need 100,000+ members, and any corridor, industry or skill needs at least 50 observed moves, so small countries and rare skills are under-represented. Restricting the tech comparison to skill groups present in at least 80% of developing economies still finds tech leaving faster in 67% of them.
  • Country, industry and skill rates use different denominators, so comparisons are made within each measure, never across them.
  • Destinations are ranked by the average share across sending countries, not by number of people.
Method

Country totals sum bilateral net flows per base country. Skill comparisons take each country's median rate across tech skill groups (LinkedIn's Tech and Disruptive Tech categories) and across all other skill groups, then compare the two within the country. Industry rates roll detailed LinkedIn industries up to ISIC sections by country median. Analysis in Python (pandas); charts are hand-built SVG.
